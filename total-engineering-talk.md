# Total Engineering

What Total Football can teach us about building with AI

Markdown planning document only. The presentation will be built later. Designed for an English-language talk to a Dutch software development team. Recommended duration: 25 minutes, adaptable to 20–30 minutes, plus questions if the event allows. A brief title view introduces ten substantive chapters, primarily titles and short labels, with visual concepts, main ideas, and presenter scripts. The Kent Beck post is the one optional exception to the minimal-text format. The talk works entirely through stories, images, and audience reflection. No live demo or real feature walkthrough is required. The [visual concept](total-engineering-visual-concept.md) develops these chapters as camera stops within one poster.

## The central idea

**Your specialty is your home position. The whole product is your pitch.**

The emotional foundation is the moment something first worked because we made it work: a page appeared, a game responded, a repetitive task ran automatically, or a colleague's day became easier. AI offers a chance to experience more of that agency across a larger part of the product.

FE, BE, QA, and DevOps become areas of expertise within a shared engineer identity. Engineers take responsibility for customer outcomes, contribute across familiar role boundaries, and bring colleagues with deeper expertise into the work when needed. AI can make that broader contribution more accessible.

Present this as an ambition for the team and a hypothesis to test. Research about AI assistance does not establish that every team should eliminate specialist roles.

The audience should leave remembering why building things matters to them, seeing a larger field of application for their experience, and knowing one next step they can attempt together. The intended feeling is: “There is something I want to build, and I can see how to begin.”

## The narrative

Begin with a personal memory of making something work. Let people recognize how role boundaries can distance them from that complete experience. Use Kent Beck's post to acknowledge the uncertainty AI brings to professional identity. Introduce Total Football as a way to think about broader contribution and mutual support. Make it personal through an idea somebody shelved because one part felt beyond their skills. Close by returning to the opening emotion, now shared across the team.

The emotional progression is recognition, frustration, uncertainty, possibility, belonging, and a willingness to try.

Three callbacks hold the story together: “It works” in the opening, a shelved idea becoming something useful in the imagined story, and the closing invitation to build something together.

Five ideas to repeat through examples:

1. Making something useful work gives the team a shared purpose.
2. Each engineer has depth and can develop greater range.
3. AI can help with unfamiliar code, draft implementations, and feedback. Engineers establish whether the result is correct.
4. Shared standards and reliable delivery infrastructure support flexible contributions.
5. Teams need time to practise, colleagues to learn from, and measures that reflect customer results.

## Slide 1: IT WORKS

**Main idea:** Reconnect people with the agency, curiosity, and satisfaction of making something work.

**Visual:** After the altered title, move into a modest first creation on a screen: a simple personal webpage, a tiny game, or a script completing an everyday task. Best choice: an actual screenshot or photograph from the presenter's history. Use one image and the words “IT WORKS.”

**Presenter script:**

> Can you remember the first time you made a computer do something and thought: it works?
>
> Maybe it was a webpage you could show someone. A character moving in a game. A spreadsheet macro that did the boring part for you. Or a script that saved a colleague from repeating the same task every morning.
>
> Take a few seconds. Picture your example.
>
> [Pause for five to eight seconds.]
>
> You had an idea. You tried something. It probably failed a few times. Then something happened on the other side of the screen because of a decision you made.
>
> That feeling of being able to change something is powerful. For some of us, it is why we started. For others, it is something we discovered along the way.
>
> Sometimes the best part happened when somebody else saw it. They pressed the button. They tried the thing. They said, “Wait, you made this?” Or they simply stopped doing the tedious task because your program had taken care of it.
>
> A few decisions you made had changed somebody else's experience. You could see the connection between an idea and its effect.
>
> And once it worked, another thought arrived: what else could I make?
>
> I want to keep that question with us today. Because alongside all the questions AI raises about our profession, it gives us new things to attempt. Things we might previously have left as an idea because one part felt beyond our reach.
>
> What would it take for us to experience more of that feeling together?

**Delivery:** Make this quiet and personal. If the presenter has a true 20-second story, substitute it for the list of examples. Include the task, the frustrating attempt, and the moment it worked. Do not invent an autobiographical story or assume everyone began coding as a child. Curiosity, pride in craft, helping people, and finding satisfying work are all valid points of connection.

## Slide 2: OUR FORMATION

**Main idea:** Specialization can distance an engineer from the complete experience of solving a problem.

**Visual:** A tactics-board composition with four zones labeled FE, BE, QA, and DevOps. A ball representing the feature waits at a boundary. The football imagery foreshadows the explanation on slide 4. Do not add invented waiting-time statistics.

**Presenter script:**

> Over time, we got better. Our systems became more complex. We developed specialties, and those specialties helped us build things our earlier selves could never have built.
>
> We also became responsible for smaller parts of a larger journey.
>
> Frontend. Backend. Quality. Operations. These names describe real knowledge. They help us find the colleague who understands something deeply.
>
> The screen is ready. The endpoint needs a change. Testing reveals a different assumption. A release detail needs attention. Everyone is contributing, and the customer is still waiting.
>
> Sometimes the moment we celebrate becomes “my part is done.” The moment when the whole thing works can be harder to see.
>
> Think of one feature that waited at a boundary between specialties. Could someone have carried it a little further with the right help?
>
> Or think of an idea you never proposed because you could already see all the teams and unfamiliar areas it would involve. The cost of a boundary sometimes appears before a ticket even exists.
>
> Our expertise can help us cross some of those boundaries. A colleague who understands a different part of the system can make a first step feel possible.
>
> That possibility matters. It gives us a way to reconnect our growing expertise with the satisfaction of seeing a whole problem through.

**Example status:** Illustrative workflow. Replace it with a real, anonymized team example if available. Treat the boundaries as a team design question.

## Slide 3: RECALIBRATION

**Main idea:** Acknowledge professional uncertainty, then give it a constructive direction.

**Recommendation on the supplied Kent Beck post:** Yes, use it here. It creates a moment of recognition before the Total Football idea. Keep it to roughly 60–90 seconds. Emphasize his final sentence. The numbers express Beck's reaction in April 2023 and should not become claims about the audience's economic worth or current AI productivity.

**Visual:** Two options for the future deck. The preferred minimal version is a large quotation, “I need to recalibrate.” with Kent Beck's name and the date. If using the supplied screenshot, crop away engagement counts and the analytics/promote controls, preserve the author, date, and both sides of his observation, and highlight the final sentence. This is the one intentionally text-heavy slide. A short reveal of the full post followed by the final sentence would work well.

**Source excerpt from the user-provided image:**

> The value of 90% of my skills just dropped to $0. The leverage for the remaining 10% went up 1000x. I need to recalibrate.

**Presenter script:**

> In April 2023, Kent Beck described this reaction after trying ChatGPT. There is a shock in it: something you worked hard to become good at suddenly feels different.
>
> He also sees a much larger opportunity for some of his experience. Both reactions appear in the same post.
>
> In his follow-up, Beck explained that he was extrapolating from a few experiences. He also said he did not know which skills belonged in either group.
>
> The sentence I want us to hold onto is the last one: I need to recalibrate.
>
> It leaves room for uncertainty and curiosity at the same time. We have invested years in learning how to do this work. It is reasonable to have a reaction when the tools change what parts of it feel difficult.
>
> For our team, that means exploring where our judgment and tools can help us contribute more broadly. Knowing which problem deserves attention. Recognizing a risky assumption. Working out whether the result actually helps someone.
>
> We can practise applying that experience in a wider area. And a Dutch football idea gives us a useful picture of how a team can do it together.

## Slide 4: TOTAALVOETBAL

**Main idea:** Flexible positions depend on coordinated coverage.

**Visual:** A physical tactics board photographed from above. Show two states with a simple reveal: one outfield player moves into an available space, and a teammate covers the area they leave. Keep player markers visually distinct. Leave the goalkeeper in goal. The only added words are “Movement” and “Cover.”

**Presenter script:**

> Dutch Total Football became famous through teams associated with Rinus Michels and Johan Cruyff. Think of the Netherlands in 1974: players moving across familiar positional boundaries, reading the game together.
>
> Here is the part I want us to borrow.
>
> [Point to a player marker.]
>
> This player sees an opportunity and moves. That creates another responsibility somewhere else. A teammate notices and covers it.
>
> [Reveal the second state.]
>
> The freedom comes from that relationship. I can move because we understand what needs to remain covered. You can support me because you can see the game I am responding to.
>
> [Trace the movement once more.]
>
> Think about how much this asks of a player. You need to notice an opportunity beyond your usual position. You need to understand what your movement changes for the others. And you need teammates who are paying attention too.
>
> That is an interesting form of freedom. It depends on the quality of the relationships around you.
>
> Now translate that to our work. If I pick up an unfamiliar API change, who can help me understand the data model? If you improve the release process, who helps verify the customer behavior? What can we trust the platform to handle?
>
> My specialty gives me a home position. It gives the team something it can depend on. And when the situation calls for it, I can contribute somewhere else.
>
> Imagine looking at the whole product with that attitude. Seeing a problem and wondering how you could help, even when its location would usually send the ticket to someone else.
>
> That is the relationship I want between our expertise and our freedom.

**Reference:** [FIFA: The watchmaker behind the Clockwork Oranje](https://inside.fifa.com/en/news/the-watchmaker-behind-the-clockwork-oranje). The software comparison is the presenter's interpretation.

**Historical note:** Call this a Dutch football revolution or an idea developed and popularized by Dutch teams. Avoid a claim that one person invented every element from nothing. The 1974 Netherlands were World Cup runners-up. If mentioning Ajax's three consecutive European Cups, distinguish Michels's 1971 win from the later wins under Ștefan Kovács.

## Slide 5: TOTAL ENGINEERING

**Main idea:** Name the shared engineer identity and show how AI can help someone enter unfamiliar territory.

**Visual:** A spacious view of the whole pitch that brings the opening title back into frame: TOTAL, crossed-through FOOTBALL, and handwritten ENGINEERING. Four small capability labels from slide 2 can appear briefly and then give way to the shared title. Keep the same completed handwriting and strike as the opening. Show the idea visually without a coding-tool screenshot.

**Presenter script:**

> That is the ambition I would call Total Engineering. We are engineers with different strengths, able to contribute across more of the product.
>
> FE, BE, QA, and DevOps describe capabilities we bring. Engineer describes a responsibility we share: understanding a problem and helping make the solution work.
>
> Suppose I have an idea for a useful tool, but I am unfamiliar with the interface it needs. I can ask AI to explain the options and help me sketch a first version. Now I have something concrete to show a colleague who understands interaction design.
>
> Or I know the interface well and need to understand the service behind it. I can ask the tool to trace the code, point me to an existing pattern, and help me prepare better questions for someone who knows it deeply.
>
> That first step matters. There is a difference between an idea sitting in your head and something you can explore with another person.
>
> The tool may misunderstand something. We may need to change direction. Our experience helps us decide what to trust, what to question, and who else to involve.
>
> Over time, some of the unfamiliar becomes familiar. The next conversation becomes easier. The next idea has one fewer obstacle before we can try it.
>
> Imagine how much more of the product you could understand after doing this together for a few months. Imagine how many more ideas you might put forward.

**Optional evidence, kept out of the default script:** A field experiment at P&G found AI helped technical and commercial professionals produce proposals combining both perspectives. This is a useful parallel to crossing specialties, with a different domain. [Dell'Acqua and colleagues: The Cybernetic Teammate](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33641).

**Delivery:** This is the conceptual reveal. Slow down for the two sentences naming the capabilities and shared responsibility. Leave enough time for people to picture themselves contributing more broadly.

## Slide 6: THE IDEA YOU PARKED

**Main idea:** Give the audience a personally meaningful reason to explore broader capabilities.

**Visual:** A photograph of an unfinished sketch in a notebook, with open space beside it. Later reveal a second image suggesting the person the idea would help: a clear desk, somebody using a small tool, or a mundane task completed. This is a human story, so avoid code, architecture, and tool interfaces.

**Presenter script:**

> I want you to think of an idea you parked.
>
> A tool you wished existed. An irritating task you wanted to automate. Something that would make your team or somebody outside work a little happier.
>
> You could understand most of it, but one part sat outside your experience. So it became a note. Or a conversation that ended with “maybe one day.”
>
> Let me give you an imaginary example.
>
> Every Monday, a colleague opens several systems and copies the same information into a report. You have watched them do it. You know there ought to be a better way.
>
> You are comfortable with part of the solution. The integration or the interface feels less familiar. You have postponed it because learning all that before you can even try sounds expensive.
>
> Now imagine making a small start. AI helps you explore the unfamiliar part. A teammate shows you a pattern that already exists. You build a first version together and your colleague tries it.
>
> They point out something you missed. You fix it. You check that the result is dependable. Eventually, Monday arrives and that little routine is easier.
>
> Picture their reaction. Something that had become an accepted irritation has changed because somebody cared enough to try.
>
> There is our opening feeling again: it works. You made something happen, and another person can feel the difference.
>
> Which idea came to mind for you? Keep it. We will come back to it before we finish.
>
> The size of the idea is up to you. Something small enough to finish can remind us how much pleasure there is in creating something useful.

**Delivery:** Tell this as a recognizable possibility. The script explicitly labels it imaginary. A true, modest story from the team would be even stronger if available later. Keep the focus on the person's experience. Leave ten seconds for the audience to think of their own idea.

## Slide 7: TEAM SHAPE

**Main idea:** People can grow their range when their team makes learning and asking for help possible.

**Visual:** A training-ground photograph showing people learning together, or a tactics-board image with covered space behind a moving player. The only labels are “Trust” and “Practice.”

**Presenter script:**

> For someone to try that, the team needs to make room.
>
> Think about the colleague you feel comfortable asking a basic question. The one who explains the missing piece and helps you understand why it matters.
>
> Their response changes what you are willing to attempt next. If asking feels welcome, you can discover the gap in your understanding while it is still small.
>
> Total Engineering needs that kind of environment. Learning time belongs inside the work. A first attempt can receive useful feedback. People know who can help them and where to find a good example.
>
> Our engineering practices provide support too. Meaningful checks and a dependable release path give us confidence to make a small change and learn from it. Deeper expertise remains available when the change calls for it.
>
> DORA's research describes AI as amplifying the strengths and weaknesses already present in an organization. That is a reason to pay attention to the team around the tool.
>
> We can choose to build an environment where someone says, “I haven't done this before,” and a colleague says, “Let's look at it together.”
>
> That is how a team gives people the confidence to step into a new part of the pitch.

**Reference:** [DORA: State of AI-assisted Software Development 2025](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/). The proposed operating practices are recommendations for this talk, not a claim that DORA prescribes this exact team structure.

## Slide 8: THE ASSIST

**Main idea:** An engineer's contribution includes what they enable other people to achieve.

**Visual:** A football photograph with both a passer and a receiving teammate in the frame, or a real photograph of two colleagues solving something together. Keep the title THE ASSIST as the only text. The image should draw attention to the relationship between people.

**Presenter script:**

> We remember the person who scores. Now think about the pass that made the chance possible.
>
> There is a version of that in every engineering team. Somebody helps you understand an unfamiliar service. Somebody asks the question that reveals a flawed assumption. Somebody builds a reliable path that lets the whole team release with confidence.
>
> If your strength is frontend, you notice the moments where an interface confuses somebody. You understand accessibility and interaction in ways that help the whole product.
>
> If your strength is backend, you see the data assumptions and failure paths that an attractive screen can hide.
>
> If your strength is quality, you know how to turn confidence into a question we can actually test. You can bring that thinking into the first conversation about a feature.
>
> If your strength is operations, you understand what happens after release and how to make routine delivery dependable for everyone.
>
> That experience can travel through the team. Through a conversation, a reusable pattern, a review, or a better tool that helps the next engineer succeed.
>
> Think of someone whose help made you a better engineer. There is probably a name already in your head.
>
> [Pause for five seconds.]
>
> We each have opportunities to be that person for someone else. As more people try unfamiliar work, the knowledge we can share has more places to make a difference.
>
> I would love us to celebrate those assists as visibly as the finished feature. They tell us something about the team we are becoming.

**Presenter note:** Avoid suggesting that everyone should carry four full workloads. A practical team experiment needs bounded work in progress and protected learning time. Specialist communities can continue to develop deep expertise across teams.

## Slide 9: OUR NEXT SPRINT

**Main idea:** Turn inspiration into a small, supported experiment.

**Visual:** A training-ground photograph or the earlier notebook idea seen again as an intentional callback. Reveal the labels “One idea” and “A teammate.” Leave the slide in place throughout the exercise.

**Presenter script:**

> Here is a practical way to begin next sprint.
>
> Return to the idea you parked. Choose the smallest useful version of it. Or choose a small product improvement you normally hand over at a familiar boundary.
>
> Find a teammate who can help you with the unfamiliar part. Agree what you want to learn and how you will recognize a useful result.
>
> Use AI to explore the code and help with a first draft. Bring the teammate in early enough to shape the approach. Deliver through our normal checks and look at the result together.
>
> Then spend a few minutes reviewing the experience. Where did the tool help? Where did it create extra work? What did we learn that another colleague could use?
>
> Our scoreboard is whether the customer can do what they needed, how long delivery took, and what happened after release. Include the time spent reviewing and correcting the draft. Also ask how it felt to carry the problem further and whether you would attempt that area again.
>
> I want you to make that concrete now. Finish this sentence: “I have wanted to build or improve ___, and my next small step could be ___.”
>
> You can write it privately or share it with the person next to you. If you share, help each other find a first step small enough to try.
>
> [Allow 45–60 seconds. Then invite one example from the room, capped at 20–30 seconds.]
>
> That is a good first training session. If this grows into a team experiment, we need to give it time inside the sprint. Learning and review belong in the plan.
>
> After a few attempts, we will have examples from our own team. We can decide what to expand based on what actually helped.
>
> You can leave today with an idea about AI. Or you can leave with something you are curious enough to attempt. I hope there is something you want to attempt.

**Optional working agreement for the team, spoken or circulated afterwards:** One primary owner and a named reviewer or learning partner. Small scope. Existing release checks. Record delivery time and rework, including the partner's effort. Share one useful pattern at the retrospective.

## Slide 10: ENGINEERS

**Main idea:** Return to the first “it works” feeling and connect it to what the team can build next.

**Visual:** The actual team together, ideally in a natural working or social moment, with the single word “ENGINEERS.” If a team photograph is unavailable, use a different archival Netherlands team photograph. Let the shift from historical players to present colleagues carry the conclusion.

**Presenter script:**

> Go back to the thing you remembered at the start. The little program, the page, the task you finally automated.
>
> Remember the moment it worked. And the thought that followed: what else could I make?
>
> Since then, you have learned so much. You have seen ideas succeed and fail. You understand users and systems in ways you could not have understood then. You have colleagues whose experience extends your own.
>
> Some of your most useful knowledge came from the times things went wrong. You recognize a misleading result. You know which question to ask before someone builds the wrong thing. You have learned how to make progress with other people.
>
> Now we have new tools to explore the parts that used to feel out of reach. There will be things to learn and habits to reconsider. There will also be ideas we can finally try.
>
> You do not have to predict the whole future of software development to take a useful next step. You have an idea, experience to bring to it, and people around you who can help.
>
> Imagine coming back in a few weeks and showing somebody what you made. Imagine their reaction when the irritating task gets easier or the thing they wanted finally works.
>
> That feeling is still available to us.
>
> That is where I want us to begin.
>
> Your specialty is your home position.
>
> [Pause.]
>
> The whole product is your pitch.
>
> [Pause. Look at the team.]
>
> We are engineers.
>
> What shall we build together?

This supports the talk's attention to the working environment around AI. It does not prove a particular staffing model. [DORA](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/).

**Why the talk avoids a universal speed claim.** A 2024 randomized study with 96 Google engineers estimated roughly 21% less time on a particular complex task using internal AI features, with substantial uncertainty and limited generalizability. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944). METR's early-2025 study found experienced contributors took 19% more time on familiar open-source tasks with the tools studied. [Original study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). In February 2026, METR said later measurements faced serious selection and measurement issues, making them an unreliable estimate of current productivity effects. [Update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/). These are different tasks, populations, and tools. Do not put the figures together as a controlled comparison or use the early result as a statement about all current tools.

## Likely questions

**“Are you saying our specialties are obsolete?”**

The proposal is a common engineer identity with different areas of depth. The experiment tests where people can contribute more broadly with support. Teams can still require dedicated specialist capacity.

**“How is this different from full-stack development or DevOps?”**

It builds on both. The emphasis is on shared responsibility across the customer journey, including quality and operation. AI may make more of those contributions accessible. The new team structure is a proposal to test, not something the tool automatically creates.

**“Who owns it if everyone contributes?”**

Name a primary owner for the feature and bring in reviewers according to risk. Broader contribution works best when ownership stays explicit.

**“Does this mean four jobs per person?”**

The experiment needs a small scope and time for learning. Track total effort, including specialist support, and check workload at the retrospective. Broader skills should help the team redistribute work sensibly.

**“What if AI produces code we cannot assess?”**

Reduce the change, ask a colleague to help establish the behavior, or choose a different approach. The person owning the change needs adequate evidence before it reaches production.

**“But I enjoy writing code.”**

That pleasure belongs in this vision too. People can use assistance where it helps them get past an obstacle or explore an unfamiliar area. The practical question is which choices make the work more useful and satisfying, alongside its quality.

**“The Netherlands lost in 1974.”**

Yes. The analogy illustrates a way to coordinate different strengths. Every approach still depends on execution and context. The proposed sprint experiment gives us a way to judge our own results.
